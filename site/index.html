The

AgeForgeAlmanac

Being a true and useful Guide to the raising of a Civilization from one fire to the far side of the stars; with a Table of the Ages, an account of Dooms and the Harbingers that cry them, observations upon Wonders, Neighbors and the Map, and four Plates engraved from the life.

Calculated for the meridian of your own terminal, and serving without sensible error for any other.

◆

Printed at the sign of the Anvil, for whoever presses Enter · MMXXVI

Chapter the First

Of the Ages

A civilization begins with a few people, a fire, twenty-five measures of food and fifty of wood. It is handed to you in that condition and no other. You type build hut, one of them rises and builds it, and from that hour the place is your concern.

The EditorThe author's own first town starved in the Stone Age. He doth not mention this.

Twenty-two Ages lie betwixt that fire and the end, and the calendar walks beside you: ten thousand years before Christ at the first hearth, the eighteen-forties by the Victorian chimneys, and at the last no year at all, only the words Beyond time. No Age will let you go until you have raised its Wonder, from the Sacred Grove at the beginning to a thing at the end the author is sworn not to name. Each is grander than the last, and somewhere about the tenth you will catch yourself proud of a railway timetable.

There are three hundred and two kinds of building, set out in thirty-nine lines, and twenty-four things to dig up, grow, smelt or quarrel over. A line climbs as the Ages turn: the hut of your first morning becomes a longhouse, then a tenement, then an arcology pod, and at the last a generation ship. You do not set workers to their posts one by one. They find their own way to work, by shares you set once and amend when something catches fire. Your office is to judge what the town needs next. You will sometimes judge wrong.

When you would know how near the next Age stands, open the Advance panel. It lays the whole march of the Ages across the screen with the year moving along it, and weighs what remains under four heads: Knowledge, People, Industry and the Monument. Behind it stand two hundred and three techs, drawn as a map in ten lanes, and your scholars work through them one study at a time while a bar fills.

Chapter the Second

Of Leaving It Alone

This civilization doth not need you in the room. Write it a plan (a hut, then a farm, then the granary you keep meaning to build, then the next Age when the stores allow) and it works down the list while you are at your own labors, or asleep, or appearing to listen in a meeting. It builds, it studies, it trades, and it does not wait up. Should you doubt the order of your list, plan optimize sets it straight for the quickest growth, homes between the workshops, and tells you how much sooner it will be done.

The EditorHe means you. You are the one in the meeting.

When you come back, the log tells what befell, in order, and you read it like a letter from home: quickly, then again properly. The ledger across the top of the screen keeps the rest of the account: the Age and the year, your people and their spirits, and one long bar to the next Age with whatever it still waits upon. Look in on it morning and evening, as you would a loaf. It is a game for people who like to have something growing somewhere.

Chapter the Third

Of the Map

No two civilizations stand on the same ground. Each game grows its own country from a seed: a river that goes where it pleases, a coast, a line where the forest gives out. The map is drawn in letters and marks, in color, and it moves. People walk to work. Smoke goes up. Later there are carts, and later still, things the author was not expecting.

The EditorThe plates are printed in one ink because the printer owns one ink. Press the button under any of them.

The old town stays on the map. The huts by the river are still there under the Victorian chimneys, if you know where to look. By the Cyberpunk Age the forest is gone and the town has set hard, like something poured. All four plates here come from one game. Yours will look different, and you will like yours better.

Should you tire of looking down upon it, turn the map on its side. The skyline draws the same town as roofs against the sky, lit at night, and lets you stand far off to see the whole of it or near enough to see the face of every building. Each Age's Wonder rises there as a landmark, in scaffolding at first, climbing as you bank what it costs. The Wonders of Ages past hang in a gallery of their own, where you may walk among them of an evening.

Chapter the Fourth

Of Dooms and Their Harbingers

From the Iron Era onward, any era may have a doom folded into it, fixed in secret on the day you enter. Most often there is none. You are not told which, and the author hath found that suspecting every era is no way to live.

The EditorHe suspects every one of them.

You do get a warning. Ere a doom strikes, a harbinger comes to say so, and every Age sends its own: a wild man gray with ash in the first, a soothsayer who reads knucklebones and goat livers and wants paying before and after, a town crier who reads out doom in the voice he keeps for tolls, a newsboy with the odds printed on the late edition, a chain email with a spreadsheet attached, and near the end a message in your own handwriting. In the early Ages not every prophet is honest. Some come with nothing behind them, and you will pay one of them, once.

Then the choice is yours. You may appease the doom with faith and culture and make it less likely to fall. You may brace, which costs you now and spares some of what would be lost. You may even invite it, if you have your reasons. When it falls, you endure it and rebuild with fewer buildings and fewer people than you had, or you let the whole civilization fall and carry what it learned into the next: its ruins, a legacy, and research that goes quicker for every era you have fallen in.

No answer is right every time, and the author hath regretted each of them at least once. At the very end waits the Last Passage, which is no common doom, and which this Almanac will not describe.

Chapter the Fifth

Of Neighbors

Eleven other civilizations share the world. You meet them by sending parties past the edge of your map and seeing whom they run into. Each has a temper: some will trade the day you arrive, and some want gifts for an Age before they will sell you so much as salt.

The EditorOne of them has not forgiven the author for something he said in the Bronze Age. He will not say what.

The mission board holds nine errands at a time, short, long and grand, each with its length, its odds and its haul already reckoned, so the choosing is yours and the dice have been thrown. On the road a party meets storms and tolls, fair weather and ambush, helpers, foes and ruins, and the ruins become new errands on the board. Now and again an offer comes marked Legendary. Take it.

After that it is the usual business between peoples: twenty-one trade routes, deals, alliances, embassies, the odd war settled by tribute, and a rivalry or two that began over something nobody now remembers. In a back room there is a Black Market, which pays the same worth whatever you take from it and goes through five times in six; the sixth time, the smugglers vanish with your culture. Ignore a neighbor and they cool toward you, and nobody tells you. The Almanac's advice is to write.

Chapter the Sixth

Of Beginning Again

Every civilization ends, and from the Medieval Age you may choose when yours does. Close the book and begin another, and every point of prestige it earned stays with you as a lasting gain to production in every Age; the farther you carried it, the more it leaves. Ages you have lived through before go quicker for knowing them, until at length they run at better than four times the pace, and what you save is spent farther on, in Ages your first people never reached.

The EditorHe has begun eleven. He has a town he still thinks about.

Some things outlast every run. Your account keeps a case of five hundred and fifty-six badges in seventeen families: one for each Age reached and each Wonder raised, a mark for every harbinger heard and every civilization met, a ladder of five rungs for each lineage of building that climbs across all your games, and some sixty-odd feats the author will not spoil. Fourteen are secret, and show in the case only as a silhouette until earned. They give points, titles, and ten of the sixteen themes that color the screen. They give nothing inside a run, which plays the same whoever holds them.

The author has begun more of these than he will admit, and hath not yet seen the same map twice.

Chapter the Seventh

Of the Instrument Itself

The whole of it is worked by typing at one prompt, as the old programs were, and the prompt finishes your words as you write them. Every panel (the research, the plan, the workers, the army, trade, the neighbors, the Wonders, the Advance, the map) opens over the full screen in the same frame, and Escape puts it away again. Type help and it lists every command there is.

The EditorHe turned VibeMode on once. He will not repeat what it called his huts.

On a Mac, which hath no Page keys, Option and the arrows serve for Page Up and Page Down, Home and End, and the minus and equals keys zoom the map. The sixteen themes run from the dark Forge to the light Daylight, with palettes for the colorblind and for high contrast among them. And for readers who would hear the chronicle in the tongue of the present day, vibemode on turns the names, the log and the stories into the slang of the young, and alters the words alone.

Chapter the Eighth

How to Obtain a Copy

The game is one small program, and it runs in any terminal you have. Choose your machine below and give it the line as written.

The forge greets you first. Press Enter, give your civilization a name, and type build hut. When you have lost an evening to it, type help. Other builds, the source, and a fuller guide to terminals and fonts are kept in the docs.

The EditorHe lost the evening. He typed help on the third day.
