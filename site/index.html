The

AgeForgeAlmanac

Being a true and useful Guide to the raising of a Civilization from one fire to the stars; with a Table of the Ages, an account of Dooms and their Harbingers, observations upon Neighbors, and four Plates engraved from the life.

Calculated for the meridian of your own terminal, and serving without sensible error for any other.

Chapter the First

Of the Ages

A civilization begins with a few people, a fire, twenty-five measures of food and fifty of wood. It is handed to you in that condition and no other. You type build hut, one of them gets up and builds it, and from then on the place is your problem.

The EditorThe author's own first town starved in the Stone Age. He does not mention this.

Twenty-two Ages lie between that fire and the end. This Almanac names four and gives a plate of each; the author has seen the rest and been told to keep quiet. No Age lets you leave until you have raised its Wonder, each Wonder is larger than the last, and somewhere about the tenth you will catch yourself feeling proud of a railway timetable.

There are three hundred and one kinds of building, and twenty-six things to dig up, grow, smelt or argue over. You do not place workers one by one. They find their own way to work, by shares you set once and change when something catches fire. Your job is to decide what the town needs next. You will sometimes be wrong.

Chapter the Second

Of Leaving It Alone

This civilization does not need you in the room. Write it a plan (a hut, then a farm, then the granary you keep meaning to build, then the next Age when the stores allow) and it works down the list while you are at your own work, or asleep, or appearing to listen in a meeting. It builds, it studies, it trades, and it does not wait up.

The EditorHe means you. You are the one in the meeting.

When you come back, the log tells you what happened, in order, and you read it like a letter from home: quickly, then again properly. Kept this way, a first civilization reaches the modern world in about a week. Look in on it morning and evening, as you would a loaf. It is a game for people who like to have something growing somewhere.

Chapter the Third

Of the Map

No two civilizations stand on the same ground. Each game grows its own country from a seed: a river that goes where it pleases, a coast, a line where the forest gives out. The map is drawn in letters and marks, in color, and it moves. People walk to work. Smoke goes up. Later there are carts, and later still, things the author was not expecting.

The EditorThe plates are printed in one ink because the printer owns one ink. Press the button under any of them.

The old town stays on the map. The huts by the river are still there under the Victorian chimneys, if you know where to look. By the Cyberpunk Age the forest is gone and the town has set hard, like something poured. All four plates here come from one game. Yours will look different, and you will like yours better.

Chapter the Fourth

Of Dooms and Their Harbingers

From the Iron Era onward, any era may have a catastrophe folded into it, fixed in secret on the day you arrive. Most often there is none. You are not told which, and the author has found that suspecting every era is no way to live.

The EditorHe suspects every one of them.

You do get a warning. Before a doom strikes, a harbinger comes to say so and waits for your answer. You may brace, which costs you now and saves some of what would have been lost. You may appease it, if you have kept faith enough to spend. You may do nothing and endure it, and rebuild with fewer buildings and fewer people than you had. Or you may let the whole civilization fall, and carry what it learned into the next one.

None of the four is right every time, and the author has regretted each of them at least once.

Chapter the Fifth

Of Neighbors

Eleven other civilizations share the world. You meet them by sending expeditions past the edge of your map and seeing whom they run into. Each has a temper: some open a market the day you arrive, and some want gifts for an Age before they will sell you so much as salt.

The EditorOne of them has not forgiven the author for something he said in the Bronze Age. He will not say what.

After that it is the usual business between peoples: trade routes, deals, alliances, embassies, and a rivalry or two that began over something nobody now remembers. Ignore a neighbor and they cool off, and nobody tells you. The Almanac's advice is to write.

Chapter the Sixth

Of Beginning Again

Every civilization ends. You get to pick when yours does. When it has gone as far as you care to take it, close the book and begin another. Ages you have already lived through go by at up to four times the pace: what took five days the first time takes two the second, and the days you save are spent further on, in Ages your first people never reached.

The EditorHe has begun eleven. He has a town he still thinks about.

The author has begun more of these than he will admit, and has not yet seen the same map twice.

Chapter the Seventh

How to Obtain a Copy

The game is one small program, and it runs in any terminal you have. Choose your machine below and give it the line as written.

Then type play, and after that build hut. When you have lost an evening to it, type help. Other builds, the source, and a fuller guide to terminals and fonts are kept in the docs.

The EditorHe lost the evening. He typed help on the third day.
